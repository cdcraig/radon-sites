Why readings swing so much within Scott County

DRAFT — human editorial pass required before publish.

Scott County sits on glacial till laid over uranium-bearing bedrock along the Mississippi — the same deposit that makes eastern Iowa one of the most radon-prone corridors in the country. That variability is the practical takeaway: county and state averages describe the odds, not your house. Two homes on the same Davenport street routinely test several pCi/L apart, because what matters is the soil directly under one foundation and how that one house moves air.

Iowa winters push readings up: the house is closed, the stack effect is strongest, and frozen ground caps the soil so gas migrates toward the foundation instead of venting. If you only ever run one test, a closed-house winter test is the conservative one.

Davenport Scott County Radon Levels — FAQ

Is every part of Scott County high-radon?
The zone classification covers the whole county, but individual readings vary sharply — by neighborhood and house to house. High-zone status means the odds your home tests high are substantial, not that every home does. The only way to place your house on the curve is to test it.
Where does the county data come from?
EPA's Map of Radon Zones sets the county classification; state health department survey data provides measured averages and exceedance rates. We cite both on every page rather than quoting unsourced numbers.
My neighborhood is new construction — am I safe?
No. Zone classification reflects geology, which does not care about the age of the house. Tighter new envelopes exchange less air and can concentrate radon. Newer subdivisions test high at meaningful rates in Zone 1 counties.
How often should I retest?
The EPA suggests retesting every two years, after any foundation or HVAC work, and after finishing a basement — and always retesting after a mitigation system is installed or repaired to confirm it works.
