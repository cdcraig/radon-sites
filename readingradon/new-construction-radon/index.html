Building radon-resistant features into a house during construction costs a fraction of retrofitting the same house later — the gravel layer, membrane, and rough-in pipe go in while the slab is still open, with no core drilling, no finished-basement demolition, and no exterior pipe to hide. Berks County sits directly on the Reading Prong — a Precambrian uranium-bearing gneiss-and-granite belt that is the single most notorious radon formation in the United States. A new Reading home is not meaningfully less likely to have a radon problem than an old one; tighter modern envelopes reduce air exchange, which can concentrate soil gas rather than dilute it. That is why radon-resistant construction is treated as standard practice by many builders in this area rather than an upgrade.

What gets built in — and the passive/active question

DRAFT — human editorial pass required before publish.

A radon-resistant new build layers in five things: a gas-permeable aggregate layer under the slab, a polyethylene vapour barrier over it, sealed and caulked slab penetrations and joints, a vent pipe run from the aggregate up through the building and out the roof, and a junction box in the attic so a fan can be added later without new wiring.

That stack is a passive system — it relies on natural stack effect and does reduce levels, but in a Zone 1 county it frequently does not get a house under 4.0 pCi/L on its own. The honest sequence is: build the passive system, test the finished house, and if it is still above the action level, add the fan to make it active. Because the pipe and electrical junction already exist, that conversion is a short, inexpensive job instead of a full retrofit.

Reading New Construction Radon Systems — FAQ

Is radon-resistant construction required in Reading?

Requirements vary by jurisdiction and change over time, so confirm current code with the Reading or Berks County building department before you pour. Regardless of what is required, building radon-resistant is standard practice in EPA Zone 1 areas because the geology makes elevated levels likely.

What does RRNC add to the cost of a new home?

Building the features in during construction is dramatically cheaper than retrofitting later, because the aggregate, membrane, and pipe go in while the slab is open. Adding a fan afterward — if the post-construction test shows one is needed — is a short job since the pipe and junction box already exist.

Does a passive system mean I don't need to test?

No — testing matters more, not less. A passive system reduces radon but frequently will not bring a Zone 1 home below 4.0 pCi/L on its own. The whole design assumes you test the finished house and activate the system with a fan if the number is still high. An untested passive system is an unverified one.

Can you convert my builder's passive system to active?

Yes, and it is the most common new-construction call. If the rough-in pipe and attic junction box are in place, adding a properly sized fan is straightforward. The house gets retested afterward to confirm the conversion brought the level below the action level.
